# Plain-English methodology (living document, v0.5)

**What is finished and what is not.** The data checks and the first build step (tidying the ball-by-ball data into analysis tables, with 50 automatic checks) are finished and can be re-run. The scoring model below is the *agreed design*; it has **not been run yet**, and no player score or ranking exists. When it is implemented this document is updated to describe exactly what was run. Concept IDs (K1-K20) match `technical_methodology.md`; the reason for each choice is in `decision_log.md`. Numbers in "cricket example" boxes are made up to show the idea unless marked **[real]**.

## The question
Who is best when the match is on the line? We measure this for batters and bowlers, using only the organiser's IPL and T20I data, and give each player **three scores: Regular-match, IPL Knockout and Overall.** (Earlier drafts called the first one "League". We renamed it because it also includes T20I series matches, which are not league matches.)

## Four kinds of match, always shown separately
**IPL league**, **IPL knockout** (Qualifiers, Eliminator, Final), **T20I non-knockout** (series and tournament matches) and **T20I finals** (only 5 matches). We show how each player did in each kind where there is enough data. The Regular-match score uses IPL league and T20I non-knockout cricket. The five T20I finals are a different, small and mixed bag (tri-series and Asia Cup finals), so they are not used to work out how IPL knockouts differ from other matches and they are never hidden inside the Regular-match score.

## Two ideas that must not be mixed
- **In-game pressure:** how much the *next ball* can change who wins. 12 needed off 6 balls with three wickets in hand is high pressure; 20 needed off 60 balls with nine wickets in hand is not. We measure it the same way in every match.
- **Tournament importance:** what the match means for the competition. We know which matches are knockouts. We **cannot** measure how important an ordinary league match was, because the files contain no points table or qualification rules, so we do not invent a number.

## Concept cards
**K1 Legal ball.** *Plain:* wides and no-balls are extra deliveries; they are not one of the six balls of an over. *Example:* an over with one wide and six fair balls has seven deliveries but six legal balls. *Why it matters:* we need the exact number of balls left to judge pressure. We checked the rule on 30,626 complete overs (4 oddities).
**K18 Balls faced, legal balls, deliveries bowled.** *Plain:* three different counts. A batter's **balls faced** exclude wides but include no-balls. A bowler's **deliveries bowled** include everything he bowls. **Legal balls** are only used to count overs. A bowler is charged for runs off the bat, wides and the no-ball penalty, but **not** for byes, leg-byes or penalty runs. *Example:* a wide that goes for four is 5 runs to the bowler, nothing to the batter, and no ball faced. A no-ball hit for six is 7 to the bowler (6 + 1), 6 to the batter, one ball faced. A leg-bye is a ball faced and a delivery bowled but is charged to nobody. *Why it matters:* otherwise bowlers could be blamed for byes or batters credited with balls they did not face. The full table is in the technical document. Three rows in the data had odd labels (two unlabelled extra runs, one "leg-bye" that is really a single). We looked at each by hand with the balls around it and handled them so that totals still add up. Two things stay unsettled and are tested separately: the data does not say what the category called "other" is (it behaves exactly like a no-ball, even down to the free hit that follows, so we treat it as one), and who is charged for extras is cricket convention, not something the files state.
**K19 Wides, no-balls and free hits.** *Plain:* pressure belongs to the *moment*, not to the extra delivery. A wide or a no-ball is not one of the normal ways a ball can end, so we never pretend it is a roll of the same dice as a legal ball. We measure pressure from the match situation the bowler is bowling from, and give a wide or a no-ball the pressure of that moment. A **free hit** (the ball after a no-ball) is different from an ordinary ball: in our data, only 4 of 918 free-hit deliveries ended in a dismissal (all run-outs), against about 1 in 18 ordinary balls, and boundaries are almost twice as common (34% against 18%). So free hits get their own set of chances. *Example (made up):* a bowler concedes a wide in the 19th over of a tight chase: the wide counts against him with the 19th-over pressure, adds a run, and does not use up one of his six legal balls. The next ball is still the same tense moment. *Why it matters:* otherwise bowlers who bowl a few wides in the death overs would be scored on the wrong kind of ball, and batters would get credit for hitting free hits as if they were normal balls.
**K20 Does skill carry across competitions?** *Plain:* we assume a batter who is good under pressure in the IPL is also good under pressure in T20Is, and the reverse. That assumption can be wrong, so we test it before trusting the combined ranking. *Example:* 87 batters have faced at least 60 eligible balls in both the IPL and T20Is. We compare how they did in each, allowing for the luck in each set; if the two sets of results line up closely we combine the competitions, and if not we also show an IPL-only ranking and say so. *Why it matters:* a judge should know whether mixing the two competitions is fair.
**K2 Value of a ball.** *Plain:* the runs a ball earned compared with what an average player would typically get in the same situation, with a penalty for getting out. *Example (made up):* 18th over, 5 wickets down. An average ball is worth 1.5 runs; the chance of getting out on a ball like this is **1 in 12**; a wicket costs 6 runs. A six is worth 6 - 1.5 = 4.5, and since the batter did not get out he also gets credit for avoiding the 1-in-12 risk: 6 x 1/12 = 0.5. Total +5.0. A dot ball is -1.5 + 0.5 = -1.0. Getting out for no runs is -1.5 - 6 x (11/12) = -7.0. *Why it matters:* a boundary in the 19th over of a tight chase is judged against that moment, not against an easy first over.
**K3 Fair comparison (expected value).** *Plain:* before crediting a player we work out what a typical player would do on that ball given the match situation, the ground, pace or spin, and left- or right-hand matchups. The same yardstick is used in every kind of match. It deliberately knows nothing about who the players are, so a player's own record is never subtracted from his own score. The quality of the opposing bowlers or batters is handled separately, alongside the players themselves (see K7). *Example:* scoring 8 off an over at a small ground is worth less than 8 at a big one. *Why it matters:* otherwise we would reward players who simply got easy situations.
**K4 Win probability.** *Plain:* the chance a team will win at this exact moment, from a simple transparent formula using balls left, wickets lost and runs needed or scored. *Example (made up):* needing 40 off 24 balls with 6 wickets in hand might be about 50%. The formula is trained only on matches that finished with a clear result, but it can be applied to any moment of any match, including matches that were rained off. *Why it matters:* it is the "who is winning" scoreboard that pressure is built on.
**K5 Leverage (in-game pressure).** *Plain:* the average amount the win chance moves on the next ball, over everything that could happen. *Example (made up, you can check the sum):* the chasing team has a 45% chance. The next ball could be a dot (30% likely, win chance falls to 33%, a swing of 12 points), a single (30%, to 42%, swing 3), two or three runs (10%, to 52%, swing 7), a four (12%, to 70%, swing 25), a six (8%, to 88%, swing 43) or a wicket (10%, to 15%, swing 30). Weighting each swing by its chance: 0.30x12 + 0.30x3 + 0.10x7 + 0.12x25 + 0.08x43 + 0.10x30 = **14.6 points**. (A sanity check: the chance-weighted average of the "after" win chances is 44.6, which is about the starting 45.) In an easy chase at 97% the same chances give swings of 1, 0.2, 0.8, 1.8, 2.3 and 5, a weighted average of only **1.3 points**. *Why it matters:* this is our definition of pressure, and it is the same in a league game or a final.
**K6 Pressure index.** *Plain:* each ball is ranked from 0 to 1 by how much it matters compared with all balls in that competition. *Example:* a ball with higher leverage than 92% of all balls gets 0.92. "High pressure" means the level of the top fifth of balls (0.90). *Why it matters:* it lets us treat pressure as a smooth scale instead of arbitrary low/medium/high boxes.
**K7 Baseline.** *Plain:* how good the player is on an ordinary ball compared with an average player, after allowing for the quality of the opponents he faced. Opponents' quality and the players' own quality are worked out together in one model, so each is judged net of the other. *Example (made up):* +0.05 runs per ball = 5 runs better than average per 100 balls. *Why it matters:* it is the "usual performance level" the competition asks us to compare pressure against.
**K8 Pressure response.** *Plain:* how much the player's results rise or fall as the pressure rises. *Example (made up):* response +0.10 per step; high pressure is 0.4 steps above the middle, adding 0.04 runs per ball. Regular-match score = 0.05 + 0.04 = 0.09 runs per ball, or 9 per 100 balls. *Why it matters:* this is the real "does he rise to the occasion" measurement.
**K9-K10 IPL knockouts versus other matches.** *Plain:* we allow IPL knockouts to differ from regular matches in two ways: a shift that applies to *everyone*, and a player's *own* knockout difference, which is only believed in proportion to how much knockout evidence he has. *Example:* everyone is expected to score 5 fewer runs per 100 balls in knockouts (made up). One batter does 30 better than his regular level over 20 knockout balls. *Why it matters:* we credit knockout performance, but a few balls cannot decide a ranking. **[real, rough]** the average knockout dip is not distinguishable from zero.
**K11 Shrinkage (borrowing strength).** *Plain:* with little evidence we lean on a sensible expectation; with a lot, we trust the player's own record. For knockouts the sensible expectation is the player's own regular-match score plus the shift that applies to everyone. *Example (made up):* regular score 9 per 100. Over 20 knockout balls he is 30 better than his regular level; everyone is expected to dip by 5, so that is 35 better than expected. Twenty balls say little, so only about 11% of that 35 is believed (about 4): knockout score about 9 - 5 + 4 = 8, not 39. The 11% is a teaching shortcut. The real calculation is done for all players at once in one model that also carries the uncertainty in the regular-match score, in the everyone-shift and in how matches cluster, so the final intervals are wider than the shortcut suggests. *Why it matters:* it stops a hot 20-ball cameo from beating a long record.
**K12 Reliability.** *Plain:* how much of a score reflects the player rather than luck, from 0 (all luck) to 1 (all skill). **[real, rough upper bounds, IPL, runs only]** at the typical knockout sample for each group: *all batters* 0.15 (about 18 balls); *openers* 0.42 (42 balls); *top order* 0.10 (about 38); *middle order* 0.18 (about 32); *lower order* 0.09 (5 balls); *all bowlers* 0.14 (44 balls); *pace* 0.13 (46); *spin* 0.08 (about 28). Openers are higher because they face many balls and differ more from each other. Roughly 100 knockout balls would be needed for a batter and 270 for a bowler to reach 0.5. Every player's table shows the reliability of each of his scores. *Why it matters:* a judge can see which numbers are solid and which are mostly luck.
**K13 Overall rating and the reference weight.** *Plain:* Overall = (1 - w) x Regular-match + w x IPL Knockout, where w is the share of pressure that happens in IPL knockout matches. We count that share by adding up the pressure (leverage) of every eligible IPL ball: w = pressure in knockout balls / pressure in all IPL balls. There are three ways to measure how much of the game is knockout: *match frequency* (20 of 371 IPL matches, 5.4%), *delivery frequency* (5.3% of the balls) and *pressure exposure* (the share of total pressure, which counts a ball in proportion to how much it can change the result). Today only the first two can be calculated; the third is calculated after the pressure model has been checked and **before we look at any player's score**, then frozen. It is not a judgement about importance, and it is the same for every player. *Example (made up):* Regular 9.0, IPL Knockout 7.8, w = 0.054: Overall = 9.0 + 0.054 x (7.8 - 9.0) = 8.9. League matches played under extreme in-game pressure get full credit through the pressure index (about 95% of the Overall weight). We will show how the top 10 changes with other weights, including the share of high-pressure balls, 10% and 20%, so judges can see nothing is hidden.
**K14 Uncertainty.** *Plain:* we redo the whole analysis many times, each time randomly re-drawing whole matches (keeping the same number of knockout matches in every re-draw), and see how much each score and rank moves. We also make sure the ranges are honest: before using them we test, on made-up data where we know the right answer, that a 90% range really contains the truth about 90% of the time, including when the data is messier than our model assumes. The ranges must also agree with a second, independent calculation. A cheaper calculation that ignores part of the uncertainty is shown only as a lower bound. *Example:* "Player X is rank 3, and ranks between 2 and 7 in 90% of re-draws". *Why it matters:* it shows which top-10 places are firm and which are close calls. A result that is "not clearly different" is reported as *inconclusive*, never as "no difference".
**K15 Eligibility.** *Plain:* players need enough balls and enough high-pressure balls to be rated, and the bar depends on the role: an opener faces many more balls than a number nine, and a pace bowler faces different situations from a spinner. We set these bars with a calculation that uses only how variable players are in general, never any one player's results, and we write them down before looking at any ranking. Players below the bar are listed with their counts but not ranked. Nobody needs knockout experience; without it, a player's knockout score is his regular score plus the everyone-shift, with wider uncertainty. Today 816 batters and 589 bowlers have at least one eligible ball; the cut-off is set after a power check, before results are seen.
**K16 What we cannot measure.** *Plain:* how much an ordinary league match mattered for qualification. We say so and do not invent a number.
**K17 Which matches are used for what.** *Plain:* we do not throw whole matches away. Each innings is checked for each job separately. *Example:* a match washed out with no result still has real balls bowled, so those balls count for pressure and for player scores, but the match cannot teach the win-probability formula because nobody won. A match with a revised target keeps its **first** innings (if it was played in full from the start) but not the second, because the target and overs changed. Of 837 in-scope matches, 802 contribute to player scores, and 771 are used to train the win-probability formula. Three matches look like shortened matches that were never flagged as such (their first innings ended well before 20 overs with wickets in hand); we leave them out, and we also run the analysis with them put back in to see whether they matter. We also check that a player really faced or bowled a ball: being listed in the team does not count as playing.

## How to read the output (planned)
For each player: Regular-match, IPL Knockout and Overall, each with an uncertainty range and a reliability; then IPL league, T20I non-knockout and T20I finals shown separately where there is enough data. The top 10 is ordered by Overall.

## From raw data to top 10 (planned)
1. Check and flag the raw data (done). 2. Decide, innings by innings, what each job can use (proposed). 3. Compute win probability and pressure for every ball. 4. Score each ball against what a typical player would do. 5. Estimate each player's baseline, pressure response and knockout difference in one model, so thin evidence is not over-trusted. 6. Combine into Regular-match, IPL Knockout and Overall with a fixed, pre-declared weight. 7. Redo the analysis many times to get ranges. 8. Test whether the ranking survives other reasonable choices.
